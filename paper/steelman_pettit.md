# Steelman of Pettit's Non-Domination, Then a Test FDK Must Survive

*A hostile exercise run the hard way. First the strongest faithful reconstruction of
Philip Pettit's republican freedom as non-domination — assuming, for the length of
Part I, that he is simply right. Then, granting him everything, the Freedom Decision
Kernel's Revocation Condition is put in front of him to see what, if anything, is left.
Three outcomes are admissible and none is assumed: FDK survives as a rival theory; FDK
survives only as an operationalisation; FDK does not survive. The verdict is argued.*

*Theory: نظریه آزادی (Theory of Freedom), Mohammad Ali Jannat Khah Doust (CC BY 4.0).
Draft for hostile review.*

## Part I — The steelman: assume Pettit is right

### 1. The thesis in one modal sentence

Republican freedom (Pettit, *Republicanism*, 1997; *On the People's Terms*, 2012;
Skinner, *Liberty before Liberalism*, 1998) is **freedom as non-domination**. You are
unfree to the extent that you live under another agent's **capacity for arbitrary
interference** in your choices. The load-bearing word is *capacity*. Domination is a
**modal** property of a relationship, not an event in it. It obtains in virtue of what
the master *can* do, not what he *does*.

Three consequences follow immediately, and each is a feature, not a bug:

- **Interference is neither necessary nor sufficient for unfreedom.** A master who never
  raises his hand still dominates; a non-arbitrary law that interferes (a tax tracking
  my avowable interests through a contestable process) does *not* dominate. Pettit thus
  cleanly separates *interference* from *domination*, the move Berlin could not make.
- **Contentment is irrelevant to status.** The slave who loves her chains is *unfree*,
  because her freedom is a fact about the standing power over her, not about her
  preferences. This is the famous result, and Pettit embraces it without flinching.
- **Freedom is a status, not a tally.** To be free is to be a *liber* — a free-man among
  others who cannot push you around — not to possess some count of unobstructed options.

### 2. Why "arbitrary," and why the remedy is not non-interference

A power is **arbitrary** when its exercise is not *forced to track* the avowable
interests and expressed ideas of those subject to it. Note the structure: arbitrariness
is not about the *content* of the act (a kind master's acts may be benevolent) but about
the *control* the subject has over whether power tracks her. So the remedy for
domination is **not** the absence of power (impossible and undesirable in any shared
life) but **non-arbitrary, contestable power**:

- the **eyeball test** — you can look others in the eye without reason for fear or
  deference grounded in their power over you;
- **discursive / equal control** over the terms on which power is exercised;
- a constitutional architecture of **dispersed, checked power** plus active
  **contestatory** institutions (courts, tribunals, ombudsmen, a free press) through
  which any decision can be challenged on the ground that it failed to track your
  interests;
- and, as the backstop that makes all of the above credible, a **secured exit** — the
  capacity to remove yourself from a relationship, which is what forces an in-relation
  power to listen in the first place.

Exit, for Pettit, is *one instrument among several* for converting power into
non-arbitrary power. It is not the whole of freedom; it is a means to the status.

### 3. Why non-domination is more fundamental than consent or welfare

This is the part most worth getting right, because it is where Pettit out-reaches both
of FDK's flanks.

**Against consent (Nozick/Rothbard).** A contract entered *under domination* is suspect
**because of the standing power**, not because of any flaw in the exchange itself.
Actual consent is therefore neither necessary nor sufficient for non-domination:

- *Not sufficient.* The dominated party may consent — sincerely, competently,
  repeatedly — and remain dominated, because consent given in the shadow of an
  unchecked power is itself shaped by that power. The verdict tracks the *structure*,
  not the utterance.
- *Not necessary.* The free citizen under non-arbitrary law may never be asked, and is
  free anyway, because what makes her free is that the law tracks her avowable interests
  and is contestable, not that she signed it.

Consent, on this picture, is a *symptom* a free person can display, not the *source* of
freedom. A theory that puts consent at the foundation has mistaken a downstream
indicator for the upstream condition.

**Against welfare/capability (Sen).** Sen locates unfreedom in the absence of
*capabilities* — resources, functionings, the real ability to do and be. Pettit's reply
is that you can be richly resourced and still **unfree**: the happy slave of a generous
master has high welfare and wide capabilities and is dominated all the same. Conversely,
a poor free-woman lacks capabilities yet is not *dominated* by anyone. Capability and
domination *come apart*, and when they do, it is the domination axis that registers the
distinctively **political** loss of freedom. Non-domination is more fundamental than
welfare because welfare measures what you *have*, while domination measures whether you
*stand* under another's power — and the second can be intact while the first is full, and
violated while the first is met.

### 4. The strongest single case (Pettit's signature cell)

A servant is legally owned by a kind master who never interferes, provides amply (her
welfare and capabilities are high), and she is reflectively content and does not wish to
leave. *Only* non-domination calls her **unfree** — because the master *retains the
arbitrary power* to interfere whether or not he uses it. The consent theorist sees a
valid consent and no rights-violation. The capability theorist sees met needs and no
deficit. Only the republican vocabulary condemns un-exercised, contented mastery. Hold
this case; it is the one that decides everything below.

**Take Part I as conceded.** Nothing in Part II disputes any of it.

## Part II — Granting all of that, what is left of FDK?

FDK's one candidate-distinctive claim is the **Revocation Condition**:

> **(RC)** Consent authorising an ongoing arrangement is valid only if the consenter
> retains a real, exercisable right to withdraw (a preserved exit right).

The question is not whether RC is *true* — granting Pettit, the protective cases it gets
right, it gets right because Pettit is right about them. The question is whether RC, or
anything in FDK around it, **adds** to a fully-developed Pettit.

### 5. RC's protective verdicts are a subset of non-domination

Take the cases RC handles well: irrevocable lifetime servitude, company towns and debt
bondage, platform and data lock-in. In every one, RC denies legitimacy because exit is
**foreclosed by an agent**. But a foreclosed exit imposed by an agent *is* subjection to
that agent's arbitrary power — it is the master locking the door, the firm confiscating
the passport, the platform forbidding data export. Each is a textbook instance of
**domination**. So:

> On the cases RC handles, **every RC verdict is a non-domination verdict.**
> Outputs: **RC ⊆ non-domination.**

RC reaches a *proper subset* of the relationships Pettit condemns, by a narrower route
(it looks only at the exit instrument; Pettit looks at the whole structure of arbitrary
power, of which foreclosed exit is one mode). RC is, on its protective range, a
*restriction* of non-domination to the exit dimension.

### 6. Non-domination is strictly stronger on the case that decides the project

Now the contented slave of §4, in the form that decides the project: **exit retained but
unwanted**. The manumission right persists; she knows it, can afford it, and declines it.

- **RC's letter is satisfied** — exit is preserved — so RC must return **VALID / permit**.
  This is the Nozickian verdict ("she consented; the exit is right there").
- **Pettit condemns it** — she stands in a dominating relationship however content she
  is, because the master *retains* the arbitrary power and domination is modal, not
  preferential.

So on the decisive case the two theories **diverge**, and they diverge in Pettit's
favour: where RC is forced to permit (or else import an interest-judgment about whether
her exit is "real for her," which is Sen, not RC), non-domination condemns directly from
the structure of power. The inclusion of §5 is therefore **strict in the worst possible
direction for FDK**: RC is not merely a subset, it is a subset that *fails* on exactly
the case that the rivals' independence turns on. Non-domination is broader **and**
strictly stronger where it counts.

This is not a marginal loss. The contented-slave-with-retained-exit *is* the empty chair
FDK was built to occupy — protection without paternalism. Pettit sits in it. RC cannot.

### 7. Is there anything a fully-developed Pettit lacks that FDK supplies?

Honesty requires hunting for a genuine residue, not assuming there is none. Three
candidates. The first two are real but not philosophical; the third is philosophical but
does not exist yet.

**(a) Consent-act granularity — a per-action gate vs Pettit's relationship-level
analysis.** FDK gates *individual boundary-crossings*; Pettit analyses *relationships*.
Could a one-off action preserve exit yet sit inside a dominating relationship — so that
RC passes the act while Pettit condemns the standing? Yes. But notice the direction:
this granularity makes RC **more permissive where Pettit is right** (it green-lights acts
inside dominating relations). A finer grain that systematically errs toward the dominator
is not a philosophical addition; it is a *liability* that has to be patched by re-importing
relationship-level analysis — i.e. by re-importing Pettit. The unit-of-analysis
difference is real, but it cuts against FDK, not for it. **Rejected** as a contribution.

**(b) Operationalisation — a machine-checkable proxy.** RC converts "is this relationship
non-dominating?" into questions a machine can actually decide at an authorisation gate:
*is exit secured? is the data portable? is withdrawal exercisable at bounded cost?* This
is genuinely something Pettit does not provide — he leaves non-domination at the altitude
of political theory and constitutional design, not as a predicate evaluable per request
by a Rust kernel guarding an AI agent's tool calls. **But:** a decision procedure for a
concept is not a rival to the concept, nor an enrichment of it. It is an *engineering*
contribution — a checkable *proxy* for one dimension (exit) of a richer property
(non-domination). Calling "we made the exit-dimension of non-domination computable for an
authorisation gate" a philosophical contribution would be a category error. It is a real
contribution, in the wrong category for the claim FDK wants. **Accepted as engineering,
rejected as philosophy.**

Worth stating precisely *why* the proxy is partial: it is sound on
agent-foreclosed exit (where it tracks Pettit) and unsound on the contented retained-exit
case (where it diverges from Pettit, §6) and silent on agentless foreclosure (where Sen
speaks). A proxy that is correct on one of three regions, wrong on the second, and mute
on the third is a useful gate, not a theory of freedom.

**(c) A consent-internal grounding of *why* secured exit matters.** This is the only
candidate that, if it existed, would be a *philosophical* addition rather than an
engineering one — and it would be an addition *to the foundations of republicanism*, not
a rival to its verdicts. Pettit **posits** non-domination as the analysis of freedom; the
inalienability of exit is, for him, an instrumental truth (secured exit is one of the
things that makes power non-arbitrary). If FDK could *derive* the inalienability of the
exit right **from the structure of the consent-power itself** — you cannot consent away
the standing by which you consent, because a normative power cannot license its own
annihilation — then FDK would explain *why* secured exit matters in a way Pettit leaves
primitive. That would be a contribution to the grounding of a verdict Pettit and FDK
share.

But this grounding is exactly the one the project's own `three_questions.md` (Q2) shows
**fails without an imported premise**. The derivation runs cleanly until it must
distinguish *alienable* transfers (I may irrevocably sell my car) from the *inalienable*
exit right (I may not irrevocably sell my standing to withdraw from authority over my
person). Drawing that line requires a premise that the person/will is **special** — and
the only sources for that premise are self-ownership (Rothbard), inalienable
autonomy/dignity (Mill/Kant), or **non-domination itself** (Pettit). The consent-internal
grounding is not consent-internal; it borrows. If it borrows from Pettit, the "addition
to Pettit's foundations" is Pettit lending FDK the very premise FDK then offers back to
him. **The one philosophical residue is empty on present resources.** It is not refuted in
principle — closing the Q2 derivation without an imported person-premise would resurrect
it — but it is not, today, something FDK *has*.

### 8. The honest verdict: what, exactly, is FDK if Pettit is right?

Lining the three candidates up:

| Candidate addition to a developed Pettit | Real? | Philosophical? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) per-action / consent-act granularity | Yes | No — and it cuts *against* FDK on the hard cases | Implementation grain, net liability |
| (b) machine-checkable operationalisation | Yes | No — a decision proxy for one dimension | Engineering contribution, not a rival theory |
| (c) consent-internal grounding of exit | Would be | Yes — but to Pettit's *foundations*, not verdicts | Does not exist without an imported premise (Q2) |

The convergent conclusion, argued rather than asserted:

> **If Pettit is right, FDK is a special-case, operationalised, consent-indexed *shadow*
> of non-domination.** Its protective verdicts are a proper subset of Pettit's
> (§5); it is strictly weaker on the case that decides the project, where it permits what
> Pettit rightly condemns (§6); its finer act-level grain is an implementation property
> that errs toward the dominator (§7a); its genuine novelty is the *operationalisation* of
> the exit-dimension into a machine-checkable gate (§7b) — valuable as engineering, not as
> philosophy; and its one possible *philosophical* addition, a consent-internal grounding
> of why exit is inalienable (§7c), does not exist without importing one of Pettit's own
> rivals' premises.

So, against the three admissible outcomes:

- *FDK survives as a rival theory* — **No.** It issues no verdict that is distinctively
  its own and correct; on the decisive case it issues a verdict Pettit shows to be wrong.
- *FDK survives only as an operationalisation* — **Yes, this is the outcome.** FDK is a
  deployable, checkable instrument that computes the exit-dimension of non-domination for
  an authorisation gate. That is a real and shippable thing to be.
- *FDK does not survive at all* — **No, and this matters.** As engineering it survives
  cleanly; the AG-authorisation use-case (deny an agent an action whose consent has no
  preserved exit) is sound regardless of philosophical novelty, *because* it is sound
  exactly where it tracks Pettit.

### 9. The one place FDK might genuinely supplement Pettit (flagged, not claimed)

Intellectual honesty demands naming the single thread that is not yet cut. It is **not**
a verdict and **not** the operationalisation. It is candidate (c) read as a *foundational*
question Pettit himself leaves open: *can the inalienability of exit be grounded in the
formal structure of normative powers, owing nothing to a substantive theory of the
person — and if it cannot, is the person-premise non-domination needs better supplied by
republican theory than by self-ownership or by autonomy?* Pettit treats secured exit as
instrumentally necessary for non-arbitrariness but does not derive its *inalienability*
from anything deeper. If FDK's consent-structural argument could be repaired to supply
that derivation without smuggling in self-ownership, autonomy, or non-domination, it would
hand republicanism a foundation it currently lacks — supplementing Pettit at the level he
leaves primitive, while still conceding every verdict to him.

That is the entire surviving philosophical task, and it is armchair work against named
opponents (Pettit, Raz on normative powers, Radin and Kant on inalienability), not a
development task. Until it is done, the hostile-reviewer-proof description stands: **FDK
is a precise, deployable, consent-indexed operationalisation of the exit-dimension of
non-domination — a strong piece of engineering and a shadow of Pettit's theory, not a
rival to it.**
