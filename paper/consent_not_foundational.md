# Is Consent Foundational? The Circularity Objection to Consent-First Legitimacy

*The most dangerous attack on FDK. The rival-comparison papers ask whether FDK's
exit-right condition is **new** ([`three_questions.md`](three_questions.md)) or whether
FDK produces an **independent verdict-column** ([`reverse_test.md`](reverse_test.md)).
This one asks whether FDK's **architecture** is even coherent: can a theory derive
political morality **from** consent, or is "valid consent" already downstream of the
concepts it claims to ground? If the latter, the project has the direction of explanation
reversed — and that is prior to, and graver than, any question about Pettit.*

*Theory: نظریه آزادی (Theory of Freedom), Mohammad Ali Jannat Khah Doust (CC BY 4.0).
Draft for hostile review. Verdict: the foundational claim fails; an operational claim
survives.*

---

## What is actually being claimed

FDK is **consent-first**. Its primitive is: an act is legitimate iff every boundary it
crosses carries the *valid consent* of that boundary's owner. The ambition is
foundational — legitimacy, and with it the rest of political morality, is to be *built
out of* consent. The exit-right condition (RC) is one proposed clause of "valid."

The three-questions analysis already showed RC is reducible to non-domination and not
derivable from consent alone. This paper attacks the layer beneath: not "is RC
distinctive?" but **"is consent the kind of thing that can be a foundation at all?"**

The objection, in one line:

> **"Valid consent" is not a primitive. Each condition that makes consent *valid*
> presupposes a prior normative concept — autonomy, competence, non-domination,
> capability, property — none of which is itself grounded in consent. So consent is a
> *node* in a web of prior commitments, not the *root* of it. A consent-first theory
> mistakes the place where prior entitlements are *cashed out* for the place they
> *originate*.**

The argument is structured on four sub-questions. For each, take FDK's own task —
specify *valid consent* — and ask whether it can be done at all without the concept. In
every case the concept is **imported, not generated**, and the arrow of explanation runs
*into* consent, not out of it.

---

## Sub-question 1 — Consent without AUTONOMY?

Can a "yes" count as **consent at all** — as opposed to mere noise, reflex, a parroted
sound, a tic, a hypnotic echo — without presupposing an autonomous agent whose *will* the
"yes" expresses?

It cannot. Consent is the exercise of a normative power: it changes the deontic situation
by waiving a claim (Hohfeld; Raz). But a *waiver* is the act of a will; a sound that is
not the expression of an agent's own volition waives nothing. To distinguish a consenting
"yes" from a coerced spasm, a conditioned response, or a manipulatively implanted desire,
you need a criterion for when a preference is **the agent's own** — and that is precisely
the problem the autonomy literature exists to solve:

- **Frankfurt**: a desire is one's own when endorsed at the second order — a desire one
  *wants* to be moved by. The wanton, who has first-order desires but no reflective
  endorsement, has wishes but no will to express.
- **Gerald Dworkin**: autonomy as the second-order capacity to reflectively identify with
  (or repudiate) one's desires; consent that does not issue from this capacity is not
  self-government but its mimicry.
- **Christman**: a preference is authentically the agent's only if it would survive
  critical reflection on *how it was formed* — screening out manipulation and oppressive
  conditioning (the historical/procedural condition).

Each is a substantive theory of what makes a volition *authored by the agent*. FDK's gate
must already presuppose such a theory the moment it treats some "yeses" as consent and
others as noise — for the line between them *is* the line between an autonomous will's act
and a mere event. Consent does not generate this line; it stands on it. **Autonomy is
upstream.**

---

## Sub-question 2 — Consent without COMPETENCE?

Validity requires the consenter be **competent**. But competence is not read off the
consent act; it is supplied by a substantive theory of agency and mind. The cases make
this unavoidable: the 12-year-old, the demented patient, the intoxicated signatory, the
psychotically deluded, the manipulated. To rule any of these "yeses" invalid — and FDK
*must*, or it endorses every monstrosity (§Thin/Thick) — the gate needs a threshold of
sufficient agency. That threshold is a claim about minds, development, and standing over
oneself. It is **imported, not generated**: nothing in the concept of consent tells you
where childhood ends or how much delusion voids a waiver.

This is conceded in FDK's *own* architecture. FDK does not pretend to manufacture
competence; it treats **authority** (graded agency/standing) as a **separate axis the
consent gate READS** rather than produces. The competence spectrum is an input the
system consumes ([`../spec/frontiers/competence_spectrum.md`](../spec/frontiers/competence_spectrum.md)).
That is an admission, written into the design, that a theory of agency is *prior* to the
consent check — the gate asks "is this party competent?" and looks the answer up; it does
not derive it. The very modularity FDK is proud of is the circularity made into
infrastructure. **Competence is upstream.**

---

## Sub-question 3 — Consent without DOMINATION (and coercion)?

Validity requires the "yes" be **voluntary / uncoerced**. But whether a "yes" is coerced
is not a brute psychological fact about pressure felt; it rests on a **moralised
baseline**. On the standard analysis (Nozick, "Coercion," 1969; Wertheimer, *Coercion*,
1987), "you coerced me" means: *you pressured me by proposing to make me worse off
relative to a baseline I was **entitled** to.* This is why the mugger ("your money or your
life") coerces but the hard bargainer ("this price or no deal") does not, and why the
*identical* proposal — "work for me or starve" — counts as a coercive threat or a mere
offer depending entirely on whether you were *entitled* not to starve. The proposition's
coerciveness is fixed by a prior allocation of rights.

So to apply "uncoerced," FDK must already possess a theory of what each party was entitled
to — a theory of rights, power, and **non-domination**. That theory is not derived from
consent; consent-validity presupposes it. The circularity is exact and unavoidable:

> rights / non-domination → baseline → coercion → **valid consent**

and *not* the consent-first direction (consent → rights). You cannot found the theory of
rights on consent when the very test for whether a consent is *valid* already quantifies
over an entitlement-baseline that consent did not produce. Note this is the deepest of the
four imports, because it is not merely that *some* prior concept is needed but that the
needed concept is the **non-domination baseline itself** — the rival FDK most wanted to
beat is sitting underneath FDK's own primitive as a precondition. **Domination/coercion is
upstream.**

---

## Sub-question 4 — Consent without CAPABILITY?

Validity requires consent be **informed**, over a **real option**. Both smuggle in a
theory FDK does not own.

- *"Informed"* is relative to *what one was owed* — which facts a party had a **duty** to
  disclose. That duty (fraud, fiduciary, contract-law disclosure) is a normative fact not
  itself produced by consent: I cannot consent my way out of your duty not to defraud me,
  because the validity of *that* consent is precisely what the duty conditions. The arrow
  runs into consent.
- *"Real option" / "exercisable exit"* presupposes a theory of the agent's **capability**
  to convert a formal option into a lived one (Sen). A nominal exit the agent cannot
  actually use — no resources, no literacy, no alternatives, adaptive preference — is not
  a real option, and only a substantive account of *capability* (the conversion of
  primary goods into functionings) lets the gate say so. Without it, "real option"
  collapses into "formally available," which re-admits the company town, the debt bondage,
  the platform lock-in that RC was built to catch.

"Informed" needs a theory of what information was **owed**; "real option" needs a theory of
the agent's **capability** to use it. Neither is generated by consent; both are read in
from a duty-theory and a capability-theory respectively. **Capability is upstream.**

## The convergent finding of the four sub-questions

| Validity condition | Presupposed concept | Source it is borrowed from |
|---|---|---|
| a "yes" that is *the agent's own* | **autonomy** | Frankfurt, G. Dworkin, Christman |
| *competent* consenter | **competence / agency** | substantive theory of mind (FDK's own "authority" axis) |
| *voluntary / uncoerced* | **non-domination / coercion-baseline** | Nozick, Wertheimer, Pettit |
| *informed* over a *real option* | **disclosure-duty + capability** | contract/fiduciary law; Sen |
| the *boundaries* consent ranges over | **property / ownership** | prior title theory (genesis regress, below) |

The pattern is exact and repeated: every place "valid consent" does normative work, the
work is being done by a concept consent presupposes. **Consent is where these prior
commitments are *applied*, not where they are *founded*.** If consent cannot be specified
without autonomy + competence + domination/coercion + capability, then consent is the
place prior commitments are **cashed out**, not where they **originate** — a node, not a
root.

---

## The quantifier itself presupposes property (the genesis regress)

Before any consent is even *checked*, the predicate ranges over *boundaries* and their
*owners*. Ownership is a prior normative structure, and — decisively — **original
ownership is not a consent act.** Whose consent legitimated the *first* owner? Homesteading,
first acquisition, the initial assignment of title: none is itself a consent transaction,
on pain of infinite regress (each consent presupposing a prior owner who must have
consented, and so back without end). So the very *domain* over which consent operates is
fixed by a theory of property that consent does not supply and cannot. FDK takes the
ownership graph as **given input** — an admission, in the architecture itself, that
property is upstream of consent. The quantifier binds variables consent never created.

---

## The thin/thick dilemma (closing the obvious escape)

A defender will try to split consent in two: maybe a **thin** consent — mere actual
agreement, a brute "yes" — is primitive, and the moralised conditions of the four
sub-questions are optional refinements layered on top. Then consent could be foundational
after all.

This fails on a dilemma with no third horn:

- **Thin consent is primitive but monstrous.** Mere agreement counts the slave's "yes,"
  the defrauded "yes," the child's "yes," the coerced "yes," the manipulated "yes." A
  legitimacy theory built on thin consent endorses every horror FDK exists to forbid. It
  is foundational and useless.
- **Thick consent is adequate but derivative.** To exclude those "yeses," you re-attach
  the conditions — the agent's own will (autonomy), competent (agency), uncoerced
  (non-domination), informed over a real option (disclosure + capability). The moment you
  do, you re-import all four upstream theories. It is adequate and not foundational.

There is no third notion of consent that is **both primitive and adequate**. The
foundational reading dies in that gap. Every gram of normative adequacy consent acquires is
a gram borrowed from a concept it presupposes.

---

## Why this is deeper than the Pettit objection

The non-domination objection ([`three_questions.md`](three_questions.md), Q1) says: *there
is a better root than consent — namely non-domination.* It is a contest between candidate
foundations. The circularity objection says something prior and worse: **there is no
consent-root to contest for.** Consent is not a foundation that loses to a better
foundation; it is the wrong *category* for a foundation, because its normativity is
borrowed at every joint. Even if Pettit had never written, the consent-first architecture
would be inverted. Pettit then turns out to be not FDK's rival but one of the *upstream
suppliers* — the coercion/domination baseline of Sub-question 3 — that consent
presupposes. The rival is underneath, holding the foundation up.

## What survives — the operational reading (and it is the honest one)

The collapse of the *foundational* claim does not make FDK pointless. It tells you what
FDK actually is, and it is something it can be honestly:

> **FDK is not a theory that grounds legitimacy in consent. It is an *operational
> consent-checker* that, given a rights/agency/property baseline supplied from outside,
> determines whether a particular act respected the consent that baseline requires.**

Read this way, every "circularity" above turns into a **correct dependency**, openly
declared: the ownership graph is an input (a property/title system); the competence
threshold is an input (an agency theory — FDK's own authority axis); the coercion baseline
is an input (a rights/non-domination theory); the capability and disclosure standards are
inputs (a Senian and a duty theory). FDK's job is the last mile — *did this action cross a
boundary without the consent the baseline demands?* — and at that job it is precise,
mechanisable, and genuinely useful. This is exactly the engineering-product verdict: FDK
as the deployable **authorisation gate** that sits **downstream** of, and presupposes, a
theory of rights it does not provide. The division of labour with an enforcement layer
(an AuthGate-style layer supplies ownership/authority facts; FDK checks consent against
them) is not a contingent integration detail — it is the *correct picture of what
consent-checking is*.

The cost is exactly one sentence the project must stop saying: *"FDK derives legitimacy
from consent."* It does not, and cannot. It **checks** consent against a legitimacy it
imports. Dropping the foundational over-claim loses nothing real and gains honesty —
and it is the description a hostile referee cannot dismiss.

## The one move that could rescue the foundational claim (and why to doubt it)

The foundational reading survives only if someone exhibits a notion of consent that is (a)
not analysed in terms of autonomy/competence/coercion/capability/property and (b) still
yields the right verdicts. The historical attempts are instructive *in the wrong
direction* for the defender — they are the strongest minds in the tradition, and each
*enriches* consent with prior structure precisely because bare agreement is inert:

- **Kant**: the contract is not actual agreement but an *idea of reason* — what could be
  willed under the universalizability of the moral law. The normative work is done by the
  prior law, not the willing.
- **Scanlon**: legitimacy is what *no one could reasonably reject* — and "reasonably"
  imports a whole prior theory of reasons and burdens. Agreement is downstream of
  reasonableness.
- **Habermas**: discourse ethics validates norms only under the *ideal speech situation* —
  symmetry, absence of coercion, sincerity. The conditions (themselves an anti-domination
  baseline) do the validating; the "agreement" merely registers it.

Two-plus centuries of contractualism converge on the same concession: bare consent is
empty, so they *thicken* it — and every thickener is a prior commitment. That is the
circularity argument restated by consent's most able defenders. The burden is now fully on
the consent-first theorist to produce the **primitive-yet-adequate** notion that Kant,
Scanlon, and Habermas all found they could not, and instead built around. Absent it, the
architecture's foundational ambition should be retired. (This is a project in the
philosophy of normative powers — Raz; Owens, *Shaping the Normative Landscape*; Hohfeld —
not in software, and its most likely outcome is a *precise demonstration that consent
cannot be normatively basic*, which is itself a publishable result.)

## Honest scorecard

| Claim | Standing |
|---|---|
| FDK is a serious engineering system | strong |
| FDK is internally consistent | fairly strong |
| FDK is usable as an AI-agent authorisation gate | likely |
| FDK is an *operational consent-checker over an imported baseline* | **strong (the survivor)** |
| FDK is a *distinct moral theory* | weak |
| Exit-right is *derivable from consent* | on the Q2 analysis, **false** |
| FDK produces an independent verdict-column beyond Pettit/Sen/Nozick | weak ([`reverse_test.md`](reverse_test.md)) |
| Consent is a *foundational* normative concept (FDK's architecture) | **false on the four-import argument (circularity)** |
| FDK is a new philosophical paradigm | very weak |

A hard political-philosophy referee will not first ask "is FDK true?" She will ask: *"What
do you explain that Pettit, Sen, and Nozick cannot?"* — and, if she is sharp, a worse
question still: *"Why think consent is where explanation **starts**, rather than where it
**ends**?"* The honest current answer to the first is "nothing yet"; to the second, on this
paper, "it isn't." The real contest is **conceptual priority**, and on conceptual priority
the consent-first thesis loses to the plainest objection of all: you cannot found a
building on its own roof.

---

## What this implies for the next step

- **Stop defending the foundational claim.** Re-describe FDK as an operational
  consent-checker presupposing an imported autonomy/competence/rights/capability/property
  baseline. True, defensible, still useful.
- **If the foundational ambition is kept at all**, the only live target is the
  primitive-yet-adequate notion of consent above — a project in the philosophy of
  normative powers, most likely to end by confirming the circularity.
- **No engineering artifact can touch any of this.** Proofs, coverage, and benchmarks bear
  on consistency-given-the-axiom; the axiom's *priority* is the question, and it is argued,
  not compiled.
