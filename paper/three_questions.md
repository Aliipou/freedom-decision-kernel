# Three Questions That Decide FDK: Reducibility, Derivation, and the Contented Slave

*An attempt to destroy the central hypothesis of the Freedom Decision Kernel, not
to defend it. If it survives this, it is worth a journal. On the argument below, it
does not survive intact — and that is a result, not a defeat.*

*Theory: نظریه آزادی (Theory of Freedom), Mohammad Ali Jannat Khah Doust (CC BY 4.0).
Draft for hostile review. The verdict is deliberately negative.*

---

## The hypothesis on trial

> **(RC) Valid consent requires a preserved exit right.** An ongoing arrangement is
> legitimate only if the consenter retains a real, exercisable right to withdraw.

Everything else FDK contains is either redundant with libertarianism or already
refuted (Parfit on standing, Ostrom on the commons). RC is the *only* candidate for a
distinctive, correct contribution. So RC's fate is FDK's fate as a piece of
philosophy. Three questions decide it. If the answers go the wrong way, FDK is a very
strong **engineering formalisation of existing ideas** — which is a real thing to be,
just not a new paradigm.

The method is destructive by design. For each question, the bar for FDK to *survive*
is set high and the burden is on RC. Where RC needs an imported premise to stand, that
import is recorded as a reduction to whoever already owns it.

---

## Question 1 — Is RC reducible to Pettit's non-domination?

**Pettit's view, stated fairly.** Republican freedom (Pettit, *Republicanism*, 1997;
Skinner) is **non-domination**: one is unfree to the extent one is subject to
another's *capacity* for arbitrary interference — power that need not be exercised and
whose victim may be perfectly content. The remedy is not welfare but *secured
standing*: institutions (constitutional constraint, contestation, and exit) that force
any power over you to track your avowable interests, making it non-arbitrary.

**The structural comparison.** Both RC and non-domination locate illegitimacy in the
*structure of a relationship* — specifically the presence or absence of a secured power
to get out — rather than in welfare or in the bare fact of an utterance. On the cases
RC handles (irrevocable servitude, company towns, platform lock-in), every RC verdict
is a non-domination verdict: a foreclosed exit *is* a standing liability to arbitrary
power. So on outputs, **RC ⊆ non-domination**.

Worse, the inclusion is strict in the direction that matters. Non-domination condemns
the *contented* dominated party — the slave who does not want to leave is still
dominated, because domination is a modal property of the relationship (the master
*could* interfere arbitrarily), not a fact about her preferences. RC, indexed to the
*present* consent and a *presently available* exit, has nothing to say here (§Q3).
**Non-domination is broader and strictly stronger on exactly the cases that decide the
project.**

**Is there an irreducible residue?** Two candidates, both thin.

- *Unit of analysis.* RC is act/consent-indexed; non-domination is relationship-indexed.
  RC could in principle pass a one-off boundary crossing that preserves exit but sits
  inside a dominating relationship, where Pettit condemns the relationship. But this is
  a difference of granularity, and on the contested cases it cuts **against** RC, not
  for it (RC is too permissive where Pettit is right).
- *Grounding.* Pettit *posits* non-domination as the analysis of freedom; RC claims to
  *derive* the exit condition from the concept of consent (Question 2). If that
  derivation were real, RC would explain *why* secured exit matters — supplying a
  foundation Pettit takes as primitive. That would be a contribution to the
  *foundations* of republicanism, not a rival to its verdicts.

**Q1 verdict.** On verdicts, **RC is reducible to (indeed a proper sub-case of)
non-domination, which is strictly stronger on the decisive cases.** RC's only
non-reducible hope is the *grounding* claim — which is Question 2. (Caveat demanded by
honesty: definitively settling Q1 requires real engagement with Pettit's texts on
*arbitrariness* and on non-dominating-but-interfering power, a category FDK's
consent-purism lacks; that engagement is more likely to widen Pettit's lead than
FDK's.)

## Question 2 — Is "consent without exit is invalid" derivable from the concept of consent alone?

This is the hinge. If RC follows from what consent *is*, FDK has a genuine foundation
owing nothing to welfare, capability, or republican theory. If the derivation needs an
external premise, FDK's claim to independence collapses into whoever supplies it.

**The candidate derivation (the strongest version I can build for RC).**

1. To consent is to exercise a **normative power**: a capacity to change the deontic
   situation by waiving a claim-right (Hohfeld; Raz on normative powers; the standard
   analysis of consent as waiver).
2. A normative power is, by its nature, a *standing* capacity of its holder. To
   *revoke* consent is simply to re-exercise the power (re-assert the waived claim).
3. An arrangement that makes consent **irrevocable** purports to extinguish the power
   *by an exercise of it* — to use the power to annihilate itself.
4. A power cannot license its own annihilation (cf. the entrenchment puzzle: a
   sovereign legislature cannot bind its successors; one cannot *promise away* the
   capacity to promise).
5. Therefore consent to an irrevocable arrangement is not a valid exercise of the
   consent-power but an attempted self-destruction of it, which the power cannot
   authorise. **RC follows.**

It looks clean. It is not, and the break is precise.

**The overgeneration objection (where the derivation dies).** Step 4 proves too much.
Ordinary transfers *are* legitimately irrevocable: I sell you my car and cannot later
"revoke" it; I waive a debt and cannot un-waive it. If "a power cannot irreversibly
alienate" were true of normative powers as such, no transfer could ever be final — an
absurd result. So RC's defender must restrict the principle: *some* exercises of the
consent-power may be irrevocable (alienating an external object) while *others* may not
(alienating one's standing within an ongoing authority-relation over one's own person).

Everything now turns on drawing that line — and the line cannot be drawn from "the
concept of consent" alone. To say *you may irrevocably alienate your car but not your
standing to withdraw from servitude over your body* requires a premise that the
**person/will is special** — non-fungible, not a transferable object. And that premise
is exactly one of three already-owned commitments:

- **Self-ownership** (the will/person is inalienable because you own yourself) — this is
  **Rothbard/Locke**. Import it and RC's verdict is Rothbard's own inalienability
  doctrine.
- **The inalienability of autonomy/dignity** (a person may not be reduced to a means or
  may not destroy her future agency) — this is **Mill/Kant**, a welfare-or-dignity
  premise of exactly the kind RC was meant to avoid.
- **Non-domination** (subjecting oneself to unchecked power is the loss of freedom
  itself) — this is **Pettit**.

There is no fourth source on the menu, and none of the three is "the concept of consent."

**Q2 verdict.** **No.** RC is *not* derivable from the concept of consent alone. The
derivation runs cleanly up to the point where it must distinguish alienable transfers
from the inalienable exit right — and that distinction requires an imported premise
about the special status of the person (self-ownership, autonomy, or non-domination).
Whichever is chosen, FDK's independence is gone: RC becomes Rothbard, Mill/Kant, or
Pettit wearing a consent-shaped notation. This is the kill. The much-hoped-for
"structural derivation owing nothing to welfare or politics" does not exist, because
the structure of consent is *silent* on whether the person is a thing — and that
silence is the whole question.

## Question 3 — Does the contented slave kill FDK? Is there a fourth route?

Setup: `P` is informed, competent, voluntary, **content**, and **does not want the
available exit**. Why call her consent invalid? Three established routes:

1. **Rothbard:** valid. Consent is consent; pity is not a defeater.
2. **Sen:** invalid — her preference is adaptive, deformed by her condition; it does not
   track her real interests.
3. **Pettit:** invalid — she stands in a dominating relationship however content she is.

The decisive question for FDK is whether there is a **fourth route** — an
RC-distinctive reason, owing nothing to (1)–(3). The candidate is the Q2 structural
argument: *invalid because consent to an irrevocable arrangement is self-undermining.*
Two sub-cases, and both close the door:

- **Foreclosed-exit slave** (the contract truly strips her exit). Here the Q2 argument
  *fires*, but — per Q2 — only by importing self-ownership (→ route 1's inalienability
  cousin, i.e. Rothbard) or autonomy (→ Mill, near route 2) or non-domination (→ route
  3). The "fourth route" is one of the three in disguise.
- **Retained-exit contented slave** (exit available, unwanted — the genuinely hard
  case). Here the Q2 argument **does not even fire**: exit *is* preserved, so RC's
  letter is satisfied, so RC must return **VALID** — which is Rothbard, route 1. To
  deny it, RC must say the available exit is "not real *for her*" because her wanting is
  deformed — which is Sen, route 2.

**Q3 verdict.** The contented slave kills the fourth route. In the foreclosed case FDK
collapses to Rothbard or Pettit; in the retained-exit case it collapses to Rothbard
(permit) or Sen (deny by interest-judgment). **There is no consent-internal fourth
route**, because Q2 showed the only candidate for one requires an imported premise that
*is* one of the three existing routes.

*Empirical confirmation.* This was not left to intuition on one case. A bank of **61
real cases** (voluntary slavery and self-binding, company towns and debt bondage,
platform and data lock-in, labor contracts, addiction, cults and adaptive preference —
[`../spec/frontiers/program/case_bank/`](../spec/frontiers/program/case_bank/)) was
scored against all four theories under a fixed rubric. **No fourth route appeared in the
61 sampled cases** — suggestive, not probative, since the rubric's buckets *are* the
three rivals (a referee will rightly say the search space was pre-carved). The
bias-corrected check that starts from the rivals' own signature cases instead of from
FDK is [`reverse_test.md`](reverse_test.md), and it reaches the same verdict by a method
not pre-loaded with FDK's taxonomy: FDK exhibits no case that is distinctively its own.
A fourth route, if it exists, will be produced by closing the Q2 derivation or by
meeting the reverse test's open challenge — not by enumerating more cases.

## The convergent verdict

All three questions point the same way:

| Question | Answer | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 — Reducible to Pettit? | Largely **yes** (RC ⊆ non-domination; Pettit stronger on the decisive cases) | No verdict-level novelty |
| Q2 — Derivable from consent alone? | **No** — needs an imported person-premise (self-ownership / autonomy / non-domination) | No independent foundation |
| Q3 — Fourth route on the contented slave? | **No** — collapses to Rothbard, Sen, or Pettit | No distinctive correct verdict |

**Conclusion, unsoftened.** On this argument, FDK's central hypothesis is **not a new
philosophical position.** It is a precise, formalised libertarian consent theory whose
one protective innovation (RC) (a) reaches verdicts already reached, and reached better,
by Pettit's non-domination, and (b) cannot be grounded without importing the very
premises — self-ownership, autonomy, or non-domination — that belong to its rivals.
The probability that FDK is a *new paradigm* is, on present evidence, much lower than
the probability that it is a **very strong engineering formalisation of existing
ideas**. That is the honest description, and it is the one to publish.

**This is not nothing.** A clean, machine-checked, productisable formalisation of the
exit-condition — usable as a real authorisation gate for AI agents — has independent
engineering and even pedagogical value, regardless of philosophical novelty. Many
influential artifacts are exactly this. But the two claims must not be confused, and the
discipline of this paper is to refuse the confusion.

## The one surviving thread (stated precisely, so it can also be cut)

If anything is left, it is a **foundational**, not a rival, project: *can the
inalienability of the exit right be grounded in the structure of normative powers
without a substantive theory of the person — and if it cannot (Q2), is the
person-premise it needs better supplied by self-ownership, by autonomy, or by
non-domination?* Answering that is a contribution to the foundations of republican and
libertarian theory, not a new paradigm beside them — and it requires reading Pettit,
Raz on normative powers, and the inalienability literature (Radin, *Market-
Inalienability*; Kant's *Doctrine of Right*) closely enough to be refuted by their
authors. Until then, the correct, hostile-reviewer-proof description of FDK is the one
above.

## What an honest next step is (and is not)

- **Is:** deep reading of Pettit and Sen; a confrontation of the Q2 derivation with the
  inalienability literature; and submitting *this* negative paper to a republican and a
  capability theorist with the single question — *"Is RC anything you do not already
  have?"* If they say no, the philosophical question is closed and the engineering and
  product work proceed on honest footing.
- **Is not:** another proof, another benchmark, another layer. None of them can move any
  of the three answers above, because none of them is about whether the axiom is *true*
  or *new* — only about whether the system is consistent given it. It is.
